For people living with HIV, dramatic weight loss, persistent fatigue, and weeks of unexplained fever can feel like the body sending an alarm — yet those signals are sometimes dismissed as stress, poor appetite, or a side effect of medication. If you or someone you love has been losing weight without trying and can't get a clear explanation, AIDS Wasting Syndrome may be worth bringing up with your care team.

## What AIDS Wasting Syndrome actually is

AIDS Wasting Syndrome — also called HIV-associated wasting syndrome or HIV wasting syndrome, and coded medically as ICD-10 B22.2 — is a serious complication of advanced HIV infection in which the body breaks down its own muscle and fat stores at an accelerating rate. It is not simply "being thin" or "not eating enough." It is a disease process driven by the immune system's prolonged battle with HIV, which disrupts the body's ability to absorb nutrients, regulate metabolism, and maintain lean tissue. The condition touches multiple body systems at once: constitutional (whole-body), gastrointestinal, immunological, musculoskeletal, and endocrine. According to Orphanet, it is classified as uncommon, affecting roughly 1 to 5 people in every 10,000. While modern antiretroviral therapy has made it far less common than it once was, it has not disappeared — and it remains underrecognized, particularly in people whose HIV diagnosis is delayed or whose treatment has gaps.

## Symptoms to look for

The defining feature of AIDS Wasting Syndrome is **unintentional weight loss exceeding 10% of your baseline body weight**. This is the required major criterion under the CDC's AIDS Wasting Syndrome definition — meaning it must be present for the diagnosis to apply. To put that in concrete terms: if you normally weigh 160 pounds, losing more than 16 pounds without dieting or increased exercise is the threshold that should prompt a serious conversation with your doctor.

Alongside that weight loss, clinicians look for at least one of the following:

- **Chronic diarrhea lasting more than 30 days** — not an occasional stomach upset, but loose or watery stools that persist for a month or longer.
- **Intermittent or constant fever lasting more than 30 days** — fevers that come and go, or that simply don't resolve, over the course of a month.

Supporting the picture is a pattern of **generalized weakness or decreased functional status** — the kind of exhaustion where tasks that were once easy, like climbing stairs or carrying groceries, become genuinely difficult.

Because the fact sheet's symptom frequency data is not fully specified for this condition, it would be misleading to attach precise percentages to individual symptoms here. What the diagnostic criteria do tell us clearly is the hierarchy: significant unintentional weight loss is the non-negotiable starting point, and the other features help confirm the pattern.

## What it's often mistaken for

AIDS Wasting Syndrome shares its most visible features — weight loss, diarrhea, fever, fatigue — with a wide range of other conditions, which is part of why it can take time to name correctly.

**Opportunistic infections** (such as Mycobacterium avium complex, cytomegalovirus, or cryptosporidiosis) can cause nearly identical symptoms and must be ruled out, because they require their own specific treatments. In fact, an active opportunistic infection and wasting syndrome can coexist, which adds complexity.

**Malnutrition or cachexia from other causes** — including cancer-related wasting, inflammatory bowel disease, or malabsorption syndromes like celiac disease — can look nearly identical on the surface. Without knowing a person's HIV status, clinicians might pursue those avenues first.

**Depression and other mental health conditions** can cause appetite loss and weight loss, and because people living with HIV face elevated rates of depression, this explanation is sometimes reached for too quickly before the physical picture is fully worked up.

**Medication side effects** from antiretroviral drugs or other treatments can also cause gastrointestinal symptoms and appetite changes, creating another layer of diagnostic confusion.

The mix-up happens because wasting is a symptom, not a disease in itself — and the underlying driver (advanced HIV with immune suppression) has to be identified and connected to the clinical picture for the diagnosis to come together.

## The tests that rule it in or out

There is no single blood test that says "AIDS Wasting Syndrome" — the diagnosis is made by applying the CDC's clinical criteria to a documented HIV-positive patient. But several investigations are essential to the process:

**HIV testing and viral load** — If HIV status is not already known, this is the starting point. A confirmed HIV diagnosis is a prerequisite.

**CD4 count** — This measures how depleted the immune system is. A CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ is one of the red-flag thresholds associated with severe immunosuppression and is a key piece of context for this diagnosis.

**Body weight documentation** — Your doctor will want to establish your baseline weight and calculate the percentage lost. This sounds simple, but it requires records over time, which is why bringing any old medical records or even recalled weight history to appointments matters.

**Ruling out opportunistic infections** — Blood cultures, stool studies, chest imaging, and sometimes endoscopy may be ordered to make sure a treatable infection isn't driving the symptoms independently.

**Nutritional and metabolic labs** — Tests like albumin, prealbumin, and a comprehensive metabolic panel help characterize the severity of nutritional depletion and assess organ function.

**Hormonal evaluation** — Because the endocrine system is affected, testosterone levels (in men) and thyroid function may be checked, as hormonal deficiencies can accelerate wasting.

The diagnosis is confirmed when a person with HIV meets the major criterion (>10% unintentional weight loss) plus at least one minor criterion (chronic diarrhea or prolonged fever), after other explanations have been reasonably excluded.

## The path to a diagnosis

Here is what a realistic diagnostic journey tends to look like:

1. **You notice the weight loss** — clothes fitting differently, comments from others, or stepping on a scale. You may also be experiencing persistent diarrhea or low-grade fevers you've been hoping would pass.
2. **You bring it to a primary care provider or HIV clinic** — ideally with any weight records you can gather. Be specific: "I've lost approximately X pounds over Y months without trying."
3. **Initial labs are ordered** — HIV viral load, CD4 count, complete blood count, metabolic panel, and possibly stool studies.
4. **Opportunistic infections are investigated and addressed** — this step may take several weeks and may involve specialists.
5. **If no other cause fully explains the picture**, and the CDC criteria are met, an infectious disease or HIV specialist applies the clinical diagnosis of AIDS Wasting Syndrome.
6. **A care plan is developed** — typically involving antiretroviral optimization, nutritional support, and management of contributing factors.

## Which specialists to see

**Infectious Disease Specialist / HIV Specialist** — This is the most important referral. HIV specialists are trained to apply the CDC wasting criteria, interpret CD4 and viral load trends in context, and coordinate the workup for opportunistic infections. If you are not already seeing one, ask your primary care provider for a referral.

**Nutritionist or Registered Dietitian with HIV experience** — Nutritional rehabilitation is a core part of managing this condition. A dietitian who works with HIV patients understands the specific metabolic challenges involved and can build a practical, realistic eating plan.

Depending on your specific symptoms, you may also be referred to a gastroenterologist (for persistent diarrhea and malabsorption) or an endocrinologist (if hormonal deficiencies are found).

## What patients go through

If you have been losing weight and feeling unwell for months — and been told it's stress, or anxiety, or "just part of getting older" — you are not alone, and your frustration is valid. People living with HIV sometimes face a particular kind of dismissal: their symptoms are attributed to their HIV medication, or to lifestyle, before a careful clinical assessment is done. Others have delayed their own HIV diagnosis for years, meaning the wasting process was already underway before anyone connected the dots.

The diagnostic odyssey for AIDS Wasting Syndrome can be exhausting precisely because the symptoms — weight loss, fatigue, diarrhea, fever — are so common across so many conditions. It can take multiple appointments, multiple tests, and sometimes multiple providers before someone puts the full picture together. That delay has real consequences for your body and your quality of life, which is why persistence matters.

## When to push for answers

Certain signs should prompt you to seek care urgently and advocate firmly:

- **Rapid unintentional weight loss exceeding 10% of your body weight** — this is not something to wait and see about.
- **Diarrhea or fever that has lasted more than 30 days** without a clear explanation.
- **A CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³** — this level of immune suppression requires close specialist oversight.
- **Feeling too weak to manage daily activities** — functional decline is a signal that the body needs support now.

If you feel dismissed, it is reasonable to say directly: "I've lost more than 10% of my body weight without trying, and I've had diarrhea/fever for over a month. I'd like to discuss whether AIDS Wasting Syndrome could explain this, and what tests we should run." Naming the condition and citing the criteria gives your provider a clear clinical question to respond to.
