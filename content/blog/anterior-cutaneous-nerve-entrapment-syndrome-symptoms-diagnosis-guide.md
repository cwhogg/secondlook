You've had the scans. You've had the blood work. Maybe even a scope or two. Everything comes back "normal," and yet the pain in your abdomen is real, persistent, and hard to ignore. If you've started to wonder whether anyone believes you, you're not alone, and your pain may have a source that standard organ-focused testing simply isn't designed to find.

## What Anterior Cutaneous Nerve Entrapment Syndrome actually is

Anterior Cutaneous Nerve Entrapment Syndrome, usually shortened to ACNES, is a cause of chronic abdominal pain that comes from the abdominal wall rather than from the organs inside the abdomen. It is also known as Anterior Abdominal Cutaneous Nerve Entrapment Syndrome and carries the ICD-10 code G56.4.

The idea is that a small nerve, one that supplies sensation to the skin and tissue of the front of the abdomen, becomes pinched or "entrapped." When that happens, it can send pain signals that feel deep and internal, even though the problem sits closer to the surface. That is a big part of why it is so easy to miss: the pain feels like it must be coming from the stomach, bowel, gallbladder, or another organ.

ACNES is considered uncommon, with a prevalence of about 6 to 9 in 10,000 people. It can appear at almost any age, with a typical peak around age 60, and it shows a slight predominance in women. Published reviews describe it as a common and easily overlooked cause of abdominal pain, which tells you something important: this is a recognized condition that is often simply not on the radar.

## Symptoms to look for

The core feature of ACNES is a particular kind of abdominal pain. The diagnostic framework describes it as chronic, localized, and burning or stabbing in quality. "Localized" is the key word here. Many people can point to one fairly specific spot on the front of the abdominal wall where it hurts most.

**The central features**

- Tenderness over a specific area of the front abdominal wall, at the site of the affected nerve. This is one of the required findings for diagnosis.
- Chronic, localized, burning or stabbing abdominal pain. This is listed as a minor criterion, meaning it supports the picture rather than settling it alone.

**Other things that can accompany it**

The fact sheet lists several body systems that may be involved, including gastrointestinal, constitutional, otolaryngological, hematological, and renal. Our source material does not break these down into specific symptoms with reliable frequency figures, so we won't guess at individual ones or attach percentages to them. What this does tell you is that the experience can extend beyond a simple, isolated ache, and that it may overlap with symptoms people associate with digestive or whole-body illness.

**A helpful clue: temporary relief from a numbing injection**

One supportive finding is temporary pain relief after a local anesthetic is injected at the suspected entrapment site. If that has ever happened to you, it is worth mentioning to your doctor.

## What it's often mistaken for

Because ACNES pain feels internal, it is frequently attributed to conditions of the digestive tract or other organs. The fact sheet notes that ACNES is commonly confused with several other conditions, though it does not name them, so we won't list specific diagnoses here as if they were confirmed.

What we can say is why the mix-up happens in general terms:

- The pain location overlaps with where organ-related pain is felt.
- Doctors reasonably begin by looking for serious internal causes, which is the safe and correct first step.
- When those investigations are clear, the story can stall, and the pain gets labeled as unexplained, stress-related, or functional, even though a wall-based cause was never specifically checked.

This is the gap ACNES lives in: the workup is thorough for the organs and silent about the abdominal wall.

## The tests that rule it in or out

ACNES is diagnosed through a combination of careful examination and exclusion of other causes rather than through a single blood test or scan. The criteria include three required major findings:

1. **Localized tenderness over the anterior abdominal wall** at the site of the entrapped nerve. Your doctor will press on the area to find a specific tender spot.
2. **A positive Carnett's sign.** This is a simple bedside test. The doctor finds your tender spot, then asks you to tense your abdominal muscles, often by lifting your head or legs while lying down, and presses again. If the pain stays the same or gets worse when the muscles are tensed, that points toward the abdominal wall as the source. If the pain eases, an internal organ is more likely. It takes a minute and requires no equipment.
3. **Exclusion of visceral causes** of abdominal pain through appropriate investigations. "Visceral" means the internal organs. Your doctor will want to make sure nothing inside the abdomen explains the pain, using whatever tests are appropriate for your situation.

The supportive finding is **temporary pain relief after a local anesthetic injection** at the entrapment site. If numbing the spot brings relief, even short-lived, it strengthens the case that the pain is nerve-related.

Notably, this means the tests that most people have already had, the ones that came back normal, are actually part of the ACNES diagnostic process. A normal organ workup is not a dead end. It is one required piece of the puzzle.

## The path to a diagnosis

Here is a realistic way this tends to unfold:

1. **Notice the pattern.** Is the pain chronic, burning or stabbing, and located in one fairly specific area of your abdominal wall? Can you point to it with a finger?
2. **Get the serious causes checked.** See your primary care doctor and have appropriate investigations done to rule out problems with the internal organs. This step is essential, and it is the doctor's job to decide which tests fit your symptoms.
3. **Ask about the abdominal wall.** If everything looks clear but the pain persists, ask directly whether it could be coming from the abdominal wall and whether a Carnett's sign test and a careful exam of the tender spot can be done.
4. **Get the examination.** A clinician checks for localized tenderness and performs Carnett's sign.
5. **Consider a diagnostic injection.** A local anesthetic injection at the tender site can both help confirm the diagnosis and offer a window into whether the nerve is the source.
6. **Confirm and plan.** With the required findings in place, your care team can discuss next steps together with you.

None of this requires you to self-diagnose. It simply gives you language for a productive conversation.

## Which specialists to see

Your primary care doctor is the natural starting point and can begin the exclusion of other causes. For the diagnosis of ACNES itself, the specialists listed in our source material are:

- **Neurologist:** focuses on nerve-related conditions and can evaluate whether a nerve is the pain source.
- **Pain specialist:** experienced in chronic pain and in procedures such as targeted injections.
- **General surgeon:** familiar with the abdominal wall and abdominal pain evaluation.

Ask your primary care doctor for a referral to one of these, and mention that you'd like ACNES considered. Bringing the name and the criteria with you can make the visit more efficient.

## What patients go through

Living with unexplained abdominal pain wears on you in ways that are hard to describe to people who haven't been there. You may have been told it's stress, anxiety, or "just IBS," or that because the tests are clear, there is nothing to treat. You may have started to doubt your own perception of your body.

There is also a practical toll: repeated appointments, repeated tests, time off work, canceled plans, and the exhausting task of explaining the same story to a new clinician again and again. Many people find themselves avoiding certain foods, activities, or clothing, trying to find the thing that triggers the pain, only to come up empty.

If this sounds familiar, please know that your pain is real and that "normal test results" and "no pain" are not the same thing. Finding out that your pain may have a recognized, identifiable source can be a profound relief on its own, whatever comes next.

## When to push for answers

Advocating for yourself is not being difficult. It is being thorough. Consider pressing for further evaluation if:

- Your pain is chronic, localized, and burning or stabbing, and you can point to a specific tender spot.
- Your organ-focused tests have come back clear but the pain hasn't gone away.
- No one has ever pressed on your abdominal wall, performed Carnett's sign, or asked whether the pain changes when you tense your muscles.

A few practical tips: keep a short log of when the pain occurs and exactly where it is; describe it in specific terms (burning, stabbing, one-spot); and ask plainly, "Could this be coming from the abdominal wall, and could we check for ACNES?"

**Seek urgent care right away if you experience:**

- Sudden onset of severe abdominal pain
- Signs of systemic infection, such as fever and chills
- Progressive neurological deficit or muscle weakness

These are red flags that need prompt medical attention, not a wait-and-see approach.
