For years, you may have been told you're just stressed, depressed, or deconditioned. The fatigue is real, the dizziness is real, the strange craving for salt is real — and yet every standard test comes back "normal enough." If that sounds familiar, it's worth learning about Addison disease, a condition that affects the adrenal glands and is genuinely easy to miss until someone knows to look for it.

## What Addison disease actually is

Addison disease — also called primary adrenal insufficiency or autoimmune adrenalitis — is a condition in which the adrenal glands, two small glands that sit on top of your kidneys, stop producing enough of the hormones your body depends on every single day. The two most important are **cortisol**, which helps your body respond to stress, regulate blood sugar, and manage inflammation, and **aldosterone**, which controls salt and fluid balance. When these hormones are chronically too low, virtually every system in the body feels it.

In most cases, the cause is autoimmune: the immune system mistakenly attacks the outer layer of the adrenal glands (the cortex) over months or years. The condition affects roughly 1 to 5 people in every 10,000, making it uncommon but far from vanishingly rare. It can appear at any age, though it tends to peak around age 60, and it affects women slightly more often than men.

## Symptoms to look for

Because cortisol and aldosterone touch so many body systems, Addison disease produces a wide and seemingly unrelated collection of symptoms. That breadth is part of why it gets missed.

The symptoms that most strongly point toward Addison disease — the ones clinicians consider most characteristic — include:

- **Profound, unrelenting fatigue** that doesn't improve with rest
- **Hyperpigmentation** — a darkening of the skin, especially in skin creases, scars, gums, and areas exposed to friction (this happens because the pituitary gland releases excess ACTH, which stimulates pigment cells)
- **Low blood pressure and dizziness** on standing (orthostatic hypotension)
- **Salt craving** — a strong, often unusual desire for salty foods
- **Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain**
- **Unintentional weight loss**
- **Muscle weakness and joint pain**
- **Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia)**

Other symptoms that appear in many patients include mood changes (depression, irritability, difficulty concentrating), reduced body hair, and in women, irregular or absent menstrual periods. Some people notice increased sensitivity to cold, or find that minor illnesses knock them flat in a way that seems disproportionate.

Because symptoms build slowly over months or years, many people adapt to feeling unwell and don't recognize how far from baseline they've drifted — until a stressor (an infection, surgery, or emotional crisis) triggers a sudden, severe worsening.

## What it's often mistaken for

Addison disease is a master of disguise. The combination of fatigue, low mood, weight loss, and vague gastrointestinal complaints maps almost perfectly onto several far more common conditions:

- **Depression or anxiety** — fatigue, low motivation, and cognitive fog are shared features, and many Addison patients are referred to mental health care before anyone checks their adrenal function.
- **Chronic fatigue syndrome or fibromyalgia** — the exhaustion and muscle pain overlap significantly.
- **Anorexia nervosa or other eating disorders** — weight loss and GI symptoms can mislead clinicians, especially in younger patients.
- **Irritable bowel syndrome** — nausea, abdominal pain, and appetite changes are common to both.
- **Hypothyroidism** — fatigue, cold sensitivity, and low energy are shared; importantly, thyroid disease and Addison disease can also *coexist* as part of an autoimmune polyglandular syndrome, so a thyroid diagnosis doesn't rule Addison out.

The mix-up happens because doctors (reasonably) test for common things first. Addison disease is uncommon enough that it rarely appears on the initial differential — yet the tests to screen for it are straightforward once someone thinks to order them.

## The tests that rule it in or out

If Addison disease is suspected, diagnosis follows a clear pathway based on established clinical guidelines. Here's what that looks like in practice:

**Step 1 — Morning cortisol:** A blood draw, ideally between 8 and 9 a.m. when cortisol is naturally at its peak. A result below 3 mcg/dL is strongly suggestive of adrenal insufficiency. A result between 3 and 15 mcg/dL falls in a gray zone that requires further testing. A result above 18 mcg/dL generally makes the diagnosis unlikely.

**Step 2 — ACTH stimulation test (the confirmatory test):** This is the key test. You receive an injection of a synthetic hormone called cosyntropin (a form of ACTH), and your cortisol is measured 30–60 minutes later. In a healthy person, cortisol surges. In Addison disease, the damaged adrenal glands can't respond — a peak cortisol below 18 mcg/dL after stimulation confirms adrenal insufficiency.

**Step 3 — ACTH level:** A blood test measuring the pituitary's own ACTH. In primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease), ACTH is markedly elevated — more than twice the upper limit of normal — because the pituitary is working overtime trying to stimulate glands that can't respond.

**Step 4 — 21-hydroxylase antibodies:** A blood test for the antibodies that attack the adrenal cortex. A positive result confirms the autoimmune cause and distinguishes Addison disease from other causes of adrenal insufficiency.

**Supporting tests:** Aldosterone and renin levels (low aldosterone with high renin indicates mineralocorticoid deficiency), DHEA-S (often low), and a basic metabolic panel that may show low sodium, high potassium, or low blood sugar.

## The path to a diagnosis

A realistic journey from "something is wrong" to a confirmed diagnosis often looks like this:

1. **Document your symptoms** — write down everything, including when they started, what makes them worse (illness, stress, heat), and any unusual cravings or skin changes.
2. **See your primary care doctor** — share your full symptom list and ask explicitly whether adrenal function has been checked. Request a morning cortisol as a starting point.
3. **If cortisol is low or borderline**, ask for a referral to endocrinology and an ACTH stimulation test. Don't let a "low-normal" result be the end of the conversation if your symptoms persist.
4. **Autoimmune workup** — ask about 21-hydroxylase antibodies, and mention any other autoimmune conditions you or close family members have (thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, vitiligo, and others travel with Addison disease more often than chance would predict).
5. **Imaging** — an abdominal CT scan is sometimes ordered to look at the adrenal glands directly, especially if the cause is unclear.
6. **Confirmation and cause** — once adrenal insufficiency is confirmed, the workup identifies whether it's autoimmune (most common), infectious, or due to another cause.

## Which specialists to see

**Endocrinology** is the primary specialty for Addison disease. Endocrinologists specialize in hormonal disorders and are most familiar with interpreting cortisol levels, ordering and reading ACTH stimulation tests, and managing the long-term hormone replacement that Addison disease requires. Ask your primary care doctor for a referral, and if possible, seek an endocrinologist with experience in adrenal disorders.

If you have multiple autoimmune conditions, a specialist familiar with polyglandular autoimmune syndromes can be especially valuable.

## What patients go through

Many people with Addison disease spend years — sometimes a decade or more — being told their symptoms are psychological, that they need to exercise more, sleep better, or manage stress. The skin darkening may be attributed to sun exposure or ethnicity. The salt craving is dismissed as a quirk. The crashes after minor illness are labeled as anxiety.

This is not a failure of character or persistence. Addison disease is genuinely uncommon, its symptoms are genuinely nonspecific, and the medical system is built to find common things first. Feeling dismissed is a nearly universal part of the Addison diagnostic experience — and it is exhausting on top of already being exhausted. Your symptoms are real, and they deserve a real investigation.

## When to push for answers

Certain situations call for urgent action — don't wait:

- **Adrenal crisis** is a medical emergency. Severe low blood pressure, collapse, intense abdominal pain, vomiting, or altered consciousness in someone with known or suspected adrenal insufficiency requires emergency care immediately. IV hydrocortisone is life-saving.
- **Severe hyponatremia** (very low sodium on a blood test) should prompt investigation of adrenal function.
- **Hypoglycemia with confusion** in an otherwise healthy adult warrants adrenal evaluation.
- If you have a known autoimmune condition and develop new, unexplained fatigue or GI symptoms, ask specifically about adrenal screening.

For the diagnostic journey more broadly: if your morning cortisol has never been checked, ask for it by name. If it comes back borderline, ask what the next step is — and if the answer is "watch and wait" without a plan, ask whether an ACTH stimulation test is appropriate. You are allowed to ask questions, request second opinions, and bring a written list of symptoms to every appointment.
